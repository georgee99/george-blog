I've always been a big Spider-Man fan, but most of my experience with the character has come from movies, games and more modern comics. I recently decided to start reading the original *The Amazing Spider-Man* series from the 1960s, starting with *Mighty Marvel Masterworks Vol. 4*, which collects issues #29–38. I wasn't entirely sure what to expect, especially considering these comics are over 60 years old. Surprisingly though, I ended up enjoying them quite a bit.

## Getting used to the 1960s storytelling

The first thing I noticed was just how different the writing was compared to modern comics. There's a lot more dialogue, and characters constantly explain what they're thinking or what's happening around them. Even during fight scenes, Spider-Man will often have several speech bubbles explaining what he's doing, which can feel a little unnecessary.

I wasn't too sure about it initially. The first issue felt pretty dated, and I was wondering whether I'd actually enjoy reading the rest of the collection. By the second issue though, I'd already gotten used to the writing and started getting into the stories. It still felt old-fashioned, but it wasn't really bothering me anymore.

I actually started to appreciate some of the exaggerated dialogue and dramatic moments. There's a certain charm to it, and once you accept that these comics were written in a completely different era, they're quite enjoyable.

## The original Peter Parker

Something that surprised me was how Peter Parker behaves in these early comics. I'm used to seeing him portrayed as a friendly, slightly awkward guy who tends to make jokes even when things aren't going particularly well. While some of that is still there, the original Peter can be quite different.

He's a lot more short-tempered and sometimes comes across as pretty rude. He gets frustrated with people quite easily, and his sarcasm was a lot harsher than what I'm used to. There were definitely a few moments where I thought he was acting a little out of character, although I suppose this technically is the original character.

Despite that, a lot of what makes Spider-Man recognisable today is already present. Peter's constantly struggling with money, looking after Aunt May and trying to balance his personal life with being Spider-Man. He also regularly finds himself in situations where doing the right thing makes his own life more difficult.

I found it interesting how much his personality has changed across different interpretations, while the problems he deals with have remained pretty similar.

## Seeing the original villains

One of my favourite parts of reading these comics was seeing some familiar villains in their original costumes. Characters like Scorpion and Kraven the Hunter have appeared in plenty of movies, games and modern comics, so seeing their early designs was a bit nostalgic.

Some of the costumes look goofy by today's standards, but I actually enjoyed that. There's something cool about seeing the original versions of characters you've known for years, particularly when their designs are still recognisable today.

Steve Ditko's artwork also grew on me as I continued reading. It's obviously very different from modern comic art, but the characters are so expressive and the action is easy to follow. I especially liked how Spider-Man's movements were illustrated, with some pretty creative poses throughout the fights.

## The Iconic Moment

My favourite moment from the collection was easily in *The Amazing Spider-Man #33*. This is the famous issue where Spider-Man gets trapped underneath a massive piece of machinery while trying to recover a serum that could save Aunt May.

What I really liked about this scene was how much effort Peter had to put into escaping. He's already exhausted, and the machinery seems almost impossible to lift. Rather than immediately finding the strength to escape, he struggles through several panels while thinking about Aunt May and what would happen if he failed.

The artwork is especially good here. Steve Ditko spends several panels showing Peter slowly lifting the machinery, which makes the whole thing feel much more difficult than if he'd simply lifted it in one go.

It's also a great example of something that hasn't really changed about Spider-Man. Regardless of which version you're watching or reading, Peter's willingness to push himself for the people he cares about has always been a big part of the character. Seeing that portrayed so well in a comic from 1966 was pretty cool.

## What still holds up today?

I think what surprised me most was how much of the original Spider-Man still feels familiar. The writing and artwork are obviously dated, but the actual stories aren't all that different from what you'd expect from Spider-Man today.

Peter's personal problems are just as important as whatever villain he's fighting. He's constantly dealing with Aunt May's health, money problems, relationships and trying to keep his identity a secret.

The villains are also still entertaining, even if some of their motivations and dialogue are a little over the top. It's interesting seeing how many of these characters and ideas were already established during the first few years of the series.

## My take

I went into this collection thinking I'd probably enjoy it more for its historical significance than the actual stories. The first issue didn't exactly convince me otherwise, but by the second one I was already getting into it. By the time I'd finished all ten issues, I was pretty keen to continue reading.

I definitely enjoyed seeing the original villains and how Peter Parker was portrayed compared to the versions I'm familiar with. The machinery-lifting scene in issue #33 was easily the highlight for me, and I can understand why it's become such an iconic moment in Spider-Man's history.

I don't think these comics are for everyone though. The dialogue can be pretty excessive, and the storytelling takes some getting used to. That being said, if you're already a Spider-Man fan and have some interest in where the character started, I think they're worth giving a go.
